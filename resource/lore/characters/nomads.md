The Nomads are a wandering people found principally in the northwestern reaches of Utopia. The name can be misleading. They do not spend their lives endlessly travelling from one temporary camp to another, nor do they necessarily move with the changing seasons.

A Nomad settlement may remain in the same place for a generation.

Families are raised there. Trade routes develop around it. Herds graze the surrounding country, workshops produce tools and goods, and people born after the settlement was established may reach adulthood having known no other home.

Then, eventually, they move.

Nomad settlements are built with this in mind. Their characteristic homes are large, sturdy circular tents constructed from hides and woven material around wooden frames. They are intended to withstand years of weather and repeated repair, but they can also be dismantled when the time comes to leave.

A large encampment can resemble a town more than a collection of tents. Homes are arranged around communal spaces, with workshops, animal enclosures, traders and places for gatherings developing between them. Paths become established through years of use. A settlement which has occupied the same ground for decades can feel remarkably permanent.

The Nomads know that it isn't.

Horses occupy a central place in their culture. They provide transport, carry goods during migrations and allow Nomads to range far beyond the settlement for hunting, trading and exploration. Children commonly learn to ride young, and an experienced Nomad rider can cross country which would make a traveller accustomed to roads reconsider the journey entirely.

Each settlement is led by a Camp Leader. Leadership is not hereditary and cannot be assumed simply because somebody possesses wealth or an impressive family name. A leader must be capable of holding the respect of the camp and, when challenged, defending their position personally.

Challenges are settled through organised fights conducted according to established custom. Victory can establish a new Camp Leader without turning a dispute over leadership into a feud between families. A leader who can no longer defend their position is expected to surrender it to somebody who can.

This should not be confused with the constant internal violence of Orcish society. Nomads do not fight because every disagreement requires bloodshed. The challenge exists specifically to provide an accepted answer to one important question: who leads.

Once that question has been answered, the camp gets on with living.

Nomads are fiercely proud of their independence. Although many of their settlements lie within territory claimed by the Empire of Man, they recognise no feudal lord and owe no loyalty to the Emperor. They pay no imperial tithe and maintain no militia under imperial law.

A Human lord may point to a map and explain that the Nomads are camped upon his land.

The Nomads may invite him to come and explain this personally.

In practice, relations vary. Trade with neighbouring Human settlements can be extensive and peaceful, and sensible lords often find commerce more useful than attempting to impose authority upon a large community of experienced riders who can dismantle their homes and disappear if sufficiently annoyed.

The absence of a militia does not leave a Nomad settlement undefended. There is little distinction between the community and those expected to protect it. When danger threatens, those capable of fighting do so. Defence is an obligation to the camp rather than a service delegated to a permanent force.

Movement is similarly undertaken by the community. When a settlement finally leaves, possessions are packed, homes dismantled, animals gathered and anything which cannot reasonably travel is abandoned, sold or traded. What follows may be a migration across a considerable distance before another suitable region is chosen.

Once there, they begin again.

The reasons for moving vary. Grazing land may have deteriorated, local resources may no longer support the population, relations with neighbours may have changed, or another region may simply offer better prospects. Sometimes the reason is little more than a growing feeling that the community has remained in one place long enough.

Trade is therefore important to Nomad life. A settlement may spend decades dealing with nearby Human towns, exchanging animals, hides, crafted goods and materials gathered during expeditions. Nomad traders and riders also travel considerable distances, carrying goods and news between communities.

Their strongest loyalty is not to a ruler or a particular stretch of land, but to the people who travel with them. The settlement can be dismantled. The community cannot.

Music, shared meals and storytelling are important parts of Nomad life, particularly during large communal gatherings. Stories preserve the memory of previous settlements and migrations: places where grandparents were born, rivers followed decades earlier, disputes with forgotten lords and distant regions which younger generations may never have seen.

For outsiders, it can be strange to discover that a settlement which has stood for thirty years is considered temporary.

For the Nomads, thirty years is simply a particularly long stop on the journey.
