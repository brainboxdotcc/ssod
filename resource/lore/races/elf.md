Elves are amongst the tallest of Cryptillia's peoples and amongst its most naturally accomplished users of magic. Their civilisation is ancient, highly ordered and deeply spiritual, with traditions reaching back well before the First Age of Magic.

Central to Elven culture is their relationship with the land. Elves do not consider territory merely something to be owned. The lands entrusted to an Elven people are regarded as a gift from the gods, to be protected and eventually passed to those who follow them. To surrender such land carelessly is therefore more than a political failure; it is a failure of a sacred responsibility.

This can make Elves fiercely territorial. Those who knowingly seize or despoil Elven lands may find themselves regarded not simply as invaders, but as heretics who have violated something sacred. Elves can tolerate visitors, neighbours and trade perfectly well. What they tolerate considerably less readily is somebody else deciding that part of their homeland now belongs to them.

Elven society places great importance on authority, but authority and responsibility are considered inseparable. A ruler has authority over those beneath them, just as the head of a family traditionally has authority within the household. Those expected to follow are expected to respect that position.

The person exercising that authority, however, is not believed to possess it without consequence.

Elves believe that the use of power will ultimately be judged in the afterlife. A father, commander or ruler who abuses those placed under their protection may escape punishment amongst the living, but death merely moves the matter before a considerably higher authority. Leadership is therefore regarded as a responsibility for which an Elf will eventually have to answer.

This same mixture of reverence and caution shapes the Elven attitude towards magic.

Elves have an unusually deep understanding of magical forces and are capable of feats beyond most other peoples. Magic protects their settlements and shapes some of their greatest works. Elven mastery has produced cities suspended in the sky, and particularly gifted practitioners are even capable of glimpsing events a short distance into the future.

Yet Elves do not regard magic as harmless. Their history gives them ample reason not to. They understand that a spell may have consequences beyond those intended by its caster, and that sufficiently powerful magic can attract forces which would be better left unaware of you.

The First Age of Magic demonstrated this on a scale impossible to ignore.

Before the Severance, the people now called Dark Elves were themselves one of the Elven tribes. They shared the same broad traditions, ancestry and understanding of magic as the other Elves. Their delegation set out with the others towards the summit at which the Peace Pact was to be established, but their ships never arrived.

The descendants of that lost tribe became the people who today call themselves the Kin. “Dark Elf” is the name given to them by outsiders, and one which carries implications the Kin themselves do not accept.

The Severance divided people who had once regarded themselves as part of the same greater Elven civilisation. Centuries of isolation, mistrust and separate experience have changed both sides since, but traces of their common origin remain. The Kin's concern with ancestry, memory and what is owed to those who came before did not appear from nowhere. Neither did their formidable understanding of magic.

They were Elven traditions long before anyone spoke of Dark Elves.

Modern Elves remain a people shaped by inheritance: land inherited from their ancestors, knowledge preserved across generations, authority inherited with obligations attached to it, and magic whose consequences can survive long after the person who cast it.

An Elf is expected to leave those things to the next generation in no worse condition than they received them.

History records rather spectacular examples of what happens when they do not.
